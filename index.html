WHAT IS DENTAL AMALGAM? Hg + powder → filling

0/1 memorized ▼
Definition — grey thick-border box Amalgam is a self-hardening metal alloy obtained by mixing mercury and a powder containing different metals in certain proportions. It belongs to the class of materials for direct permanent filling.
Why it matters
  • One of the oldest restorative materials in dentistry — the benchmark for comparison with modern restorative materials
  • Main purpose: restore defects in the chewing group of teeth 🦷 molar zone
  • Black (1896) in his classification and cavity-preparation rules focused on amalgam as the main restorative material

COMPOSITION — what each metal does Hg · Ag · Sn · Cu · Zn

0/1 memorized ▼
Orange — the basic formula Any amalgam is a two-component system: liquid (mercury) + powder of different metals. Ratio ≈ 43–54% mercury : 57–46% powder. Most properties are determined by the powder composition.
Metal“+” (green pen)“−” (red pen)
Mercury (Hg)the actual primary agent causing the chemical reactionexcess (more than necessary) reduces strength; causes toxic and allergic reactions
Copper (Cu)gives strength; basis of corrosion protection; antibacterial propertiesincreases volumetric expansion; accelerates curing
Silver (Ag)gives strength; protects against corrosioncauses volumetric expansion of amalgam
Tin (Sn)slows hardening; increases plasticityreduces strength
Zinc (Zn)increases mercury wettability of components during alloy preparation— (but see the zinc–water warning below)
⚠ Zinc + water = hydrogen! With zinc-containing amalgams, complete dryness is necessary: zinc reacts with water → actively releases hydrogen → formation of pores → expansion of the filling with possible violation of marginal adhesion. ⊘💧 Zn + H₂O → H₂ ↑

PROPERTIES OF AMALGAM 4 groups + big “+/−”

0/1 memorized ▼
Four property groups (black bullets)
  • 1. Physical: high compressive & tensile strength · high radiopacity · good wear resistance · no significant volumetric changes · high thermal and electrical conductivity
  • 2. Chemical: certain corrosion resistance · stability of internal structure (phases) · presence of residual (unreacted) mercury · reaction with oral fluid → possible staining of filling and tooth tissue
  • 3. Biological: tolerance to oral mucosa and dental pulp
  • 4. Operational: convenience & speed · sufficient working time · broad indications, universal application · easy polishing, long-lasting mirror effect

✓ Advantages “+” (7)

  • Durability — average lifespan about 10 years
  • Low cost compared with other filling materials
  • Simple, fast application technique, low working-condition demands
  • Wide range of clinical situations
  • Can repair large defects in a single visit
  • Self-sealing effect at the filling–tooth interface
  • Easy restoration when correcting old fillings

✗ Disadvantages “−” (6)

  • Rigorous cavity design — often requires removal of healthy tissue
  • No adhesion to tooth tissue → possible marginal adhesion failure
  • Does not meet aesthetic requirements
  • Low corrosion resistance of traditional amalgams
  • High thermal & electrical conductivity → possible galvanic effects
  • Mercury content → additional waste management measures

INDICATIONS & CONTRAINDICATIONS

0/1 memorized ▼
Green — indications for modern amalgams (6)
  • Medium to large carious defects of classes I, II and V in deciduous and permanent teeth, including areas with high chewing loads
  • Class V cavities in molars and premolars without high aesthetic requirements
  • Restoration of a tooth stump under artificial metal and metal-ceramic crowns
  • Patients with inadequate hygiene
  • When it is difficult or impossible to control dryness of the working field
  • When price is the determining factor in choosing the filling material
⚠ Red — contraindications (2 general + 7 for silver amalgam) General:
  • Allergy to mercury or any metal in the powder
  • Areas visible in the smile where high aesthetics is the main requirement (e.g. anterior teeth — risk of serious staining of tooth tissue)
For silver amalgam additionally:
  • Presence of gold structures in the oral cavity
  • Teeth used for metal orthopaedic constructions
  • Filling of the frontal group of teeth
  • Radiation therapy in the maxillofacial region
  • Cavities with thin walls
  • Allergy to amalgam components
  • Diseases of the oral mucosa

CHEMICAL COMPOSITION & CURING REACTION — the phases γ · γ1 · γ2 · η

0/1 memorized ▼
PhaseWhat it isFormula
γ (gamma)main component of the powder BEFORE mixing with mercury — intermetalloid of silver + tinAg₃Sn
γ1 (gamma-1)compound of silver + mercury, formed by amalgamationAg₂Hg₃
γ2 (gamma-2)compound of tin + mercury, formed by amalgamation — the weak, corrosion-prone phaseSn₈Hg
η (eta / h-phase)result of copper + tin interaction in MODERN amalgamsCu₆Sn₅
Traditional vs modern amalgam
Traditional amalgamModern amalgam
Definitionup to 95% of powder = silver + tin; after solidification contains γ2-phasedue to higher copper content, does NOT contain γ2 phase after solidification
Synonymsconventionalcopper-rich amalgam, amalgam without γ2-phase
CompositionAg 65–75% · Sn 23–33% · Cu 2–8% + traces (zinc, lead)Cu minimum 12%, up to 30% · Ag 40–80% · Sn 17–32%
  • USSR example: SSTA-0.1 (TU 64-2-43-75): silver 66%, tin 32%, copper 2%
How the curing reaction runs ⚗ crystallisation nuclei
  1. The reaction of interaction of amalgam components proceeds initially in a uniform way
  2. During amalgamation, crystallisation nuclei are formed
  3. They form a matrix of interaction products of silver, tin and mercury (γ1 + γ2 phases)
  4. Not all Ag₃Sn (γ-phase) particles react with mercury — the unreacted particles act as filler
  5. In modern amalgams, copper does NOT interact with mercury but actively reacts with tin → intermetallic alloy Cu₆Sn₅ (η-phase), crystals distributed in the material
Definition — grey thick-border box Amalgamation is the automatic process of mixing the basic components — mercury and powder — to form a plastic, self-hardening mass suitable for filling dental hard tissue defects.
⚠ Do NOT mix by hand Manual mixing of amalgam is not currently used due to the high risk of mercury intoxication. ⊘ hand mixing
⚠ Purple — particle size logic The smaller the particles → the faster the setting — but a large amount of free, unreacted mercury remains.

CAVITY PREPARATION RULES box-shape · retention

0/1 memorized ▼
Orange — why the design is rigorous Amalgam has no adhesion to tooth tissue → the cavity must be box-shaped with retention points and walls of sufficient thickness. This often requires additional removal of healthy tissue.
Shape rules
  • Box-shaped cavity with a flat bottom perpendicular to the direction of masticatory pressure, and plumb walls
  • Remove weakened cusps without underlying dentin, unsupported enamel on the gingival wall
  • No sharp angles between walls and bottom — round them with pear-shaped burs (prevents wall fractures and marginal adhesion violation)
  • Generation I (low-silver) amalgam → angle bottom–walls = 90°
  • Generations II–III (high-copper, without γ2) → walls slightly convergent, angle ≈ 70° (improves mechanical retention)
Internal wall design (6 rules)
  1. Bottom must have no sharp corners
  2. Height : width ratio not less than 2:3; protrusions can reinforce
  3. Additional retentions to hold the amalgam
  4. Enamel edges bevelled at 45°
  5. Remove carious tissue, extend boundaries to immune zones
  6. Remove enamel without dentin support and overhanging enamel edges
Minimum depth & linings
  • Cavity formed within enamel + superficial dentin, at least 1–1.5 mm deep (even excising healthy tissue)
  • In areas of greater chewing load → filling at least 2 mm thick
  • Amalgam is thermally & electrically conductive → cover dentin over the whole cavity area with an insulating pad 0.1–0.3 mm thick (any suitable cement)
  • Walls can be coated with special varnishes — antibacterial, prevent bacterial invasion at the filling–tooth interface
  • Best choice for the isolation seal: glass ionomer cements (chemical adhesion to tooth tissue, good biological & physical properties)
  • Class II cavities → isolate with a wedge-fixed matrix (avoids extrusion into the interdental space, helps form the filling, creates a tight contact point)
⚠ Do NOT Do not make the lining too thick — it may affect the strength of the filling.

WORKING TECHNIQUE — mixer, gun, condensation, capsules ⏱ timing is everything

0/1 memorized ▼
Minimal instrument set before filling
  • Amalgam mixer · gun · 1–3 corkscrews (various sizes) · 1–2 carvers (different shapes)
Amalgam mixer oscillations/min + amplitude
  • Machine for automatic mixing of components dosed in capsules
  • Main characteristic = mixing speed: number of oscillations per minute + amplitude
  • Vibration amplitude of most machines ≈ 5 cm; frequency 2000–4000
Oscillation frequencyAmalgamation rate
2000–2800low
2800–3500medium
> 3500high
Instruments for placement & condensation
  • Amalgam gun — introduces portions into the cavity; straight, curved, single- and double-sided; lever-type and plunger-type (syringe-like) carriers
  • Amalgam corkscrew — cylindrical tool with a flat working part for condensing the plastic mass
  • Amalgamotreger — a corkscrew with cross-shaped notches; packs and rubs amalgam against the walls
  • Condensation force: spherical particles → less pressure; needle-shaped → more pressure
  • First portion (lining still soft) → rub thoroughly against walls & bottom with a corkscrew with a ball at the end
Definition — grey thick-border box Condensation (packing) is the stage of compacting the introduced portions of amalgam in the cavity with a special corkscrew to obtain a monolithic, poreless mass tightly adhering to the cavity walls.
Capsules
  • Capsule = single-use release form that minimises contact with pure mercury
  • Powder : mercury ratio ≈ 1.0 : 0.85 – 1.0 : 0.96
  • Active capsules: one section; powder + mercury in a thin polyethylene bag — mixer vibration tears the bag, components mix
  • Passive capsules: two sections (mercury ball + powder) — must be activated first (press piston or rotate sections) so mercury contacts powder, then mixed
  • Amount of alloy coded by numbers 1–5 or by lid colour (like endodontic instruments); some makers colour-code setting rate, numerically code volume
  • Sizing: premolar class I/II small-medium → one capsule №1 or №2; molars → 2 or more №2/№3; №2 used most often; large №5 rarely — mainly for MOD cavities
  • Keep an assortment of capsules → saves material, reduces waste
Key definitions of time
Definitions — grey boxes Setting time = interval from mixing to loss of plasticity (after which it can no longer be condensed). Modern amalgams: 3.5 min (fast-setting) to 6 min (slow-setting). More mercury → longer setting.

Carving time = from loss of plasticity to curing, when the carver can model the filling and remove excess.
Mixing & filling sequence
  1. Set mixing time; insert capsule into clip; activate if needed; close cover; switch on mixer. Mixing usually takes 8–12 s
  2. Open capsule, extract amalgam onto a flat surface (glass, kraft paper, mixing palette); special capsule stands help pick up portions
  3. Introduce several small portions with the gun; carefully condense each with a corkscrew so material adheres tightly to walls; subsequent portions may be larger
  4. Condense with medium force in most cases; increase force only with many needle-like particles; spherical → low-to-medium force
  5. Adequate condensation removes unreacted mercury, gives a monolithic poreless filling
  6. Apply material with a small surplus — simplifies modelling, avoids adding more material
⚠ Purple — timing numbers Standard operating time for filling + condensation: 3.5–5 min. Class II: matrix removed no sooner than 5 min, gentle vestibular-oral movements; remove large surpluses BEFORE the matrix. Significant reduction or increase of amalgamation time → worse properties + more residual mercury.
  • Criteria of unsuitable mixed amalgam: brittleness, loss of lustre
  • Dryness during condensation is desirable, but if blood/saliva/gingival fluid gets between portions, drying with an air jet is sufficient — complete dryness only for zinc-containing amalgams

CARVING, FINISHING & POLISHING cut toward the tooth!

0/1 memorized ▼
Definition — grey thick-border box Carving is the process of modelling a filling by removing (cutting off) excess amalgam with a carver after it has set. A carver is a tool with a working part of various shapes and sharp edges for cutting off excess amalgam.
⚠ The golden rule Always cut towards the hard tissue of the tooth!!!
Carvers (named tools, black list)
  • Akers · Frahm · Hollenback · for interproximal surfaces · Reeves · Vehe · Le Cron · Levy · Martin · Shoshan · Walls · Ward
Modelling an amalgam filling (4 steps)
  1. Trim the edge of the filling with a Hollenbach instrument, cut at 45°
  2. Remove the matrix from the tooth
  3. Remove excess amalgam from the interdental space
  4. Model the masticatory surfaces based on the cusps (Le Cron)
Carving timing & technique
  • Average carving time ≈ 6 min
  • First remove excess from the marginal ridge and filling–tooth interface with sliding movements from filling to enamel (prevents chipping)
  • Movements precise, little force — in the first 10 min amalgam is easy to work with but still brittle
  • After rough contouring, model the occlusal surface starting from the central fissure, mesial & distal fossa
  • Transitions & contact surfaces → sickle-shaped carvers; excess → removed with floss
  • Check occlusion with occlusal paper: first vertical, then lateral movements
  • Signs carving time is over: metallic creaking sound + need for considerable force to cut
  • After 15–20 min from mixing, excess can usually only be removed with a polishing bur
Finishing & polishing — two options (both equally effective)
Definition — grey box Burnisher — a tool of various shapes with rounded edges for smooth transitions at the filling–tooth border and gloss at the finishing stage. Burnishing = smoothing the amalgam surface after carving with a burnisher and special polishers.

Option 1 — Classic (two visits)

  • 1st visit: smooth surface with a burnisher
  • After 24 h: finish with silicone polishers + brushes with polishing paste
  • Advantages: better appearance; no mercury-rich surface layer

Option 2 — Immediate (one visit)

  • Burnishing + polishing carried out immediately
  • Advantage: work completed in one visit
  • Recommended for amalgams with spherical particles
  • Amalgam polishers = silicone heads for the contra-angle handpiece: brown first (more abrasive), green for final polishing
  • Special finishing burs, silicone polishers, brushes with pastes; contact surfaces → medium & small abrasive strips
  • Modern high-copper amalgams darken over time → restore lustre by polishing with brush + paste
  • Goal of finishing: smoothest, brightest surface resistant to plaque adhesion and corrosion
  • Amalgams are polished at the next visit (long crystallisation time), with special smooth metallic polishers resembling spherical burs, fixed in the contra-angle handpiece

ADHESIVE TECHNIQUE FOR AMALGAM 2 variants

0/1 memorized ▼
Orange — why it exists The adhesive technique compensates for amalgam’s disadvantages: an “intermediary” adhesive system eliminates lack of adhesion, heat & electrical conductivity problems, and reduces the need for additional retention points → preserves more healthy tooth tissue. Preparation, condensation and processing are identical to the classical technique — the difference is in tooth preparation before amalgam application.

✓ Pros

  • Compensates for no adhesion, thermal & electrical conductivity
  • Fewer retention points → saves healthy tissue
  • Better marginal adhesion

✗ Cons

  • Higher cost
  • Complex, demanding to technique conditions
  • Contamination of the working field can negate all advantages
⚠ Purple — the honest caveat Any enamel-dentin adhesive system bonds amalgam chemically (amalgam metals interact with carboxyl groups of the oxygen-inhibited layer of the cured adhesive). But this is only “conditionally adhesive”: bonding force often does not exceed 7 MPa — clearly insufficient for amalgam’s clinical situations. The system acts mainly as an insulating spacer improving marginal adhesion. Special 4th–6th generation systems give >20 MPa to dentin.
Variant 1 — adhesive systems of the 4th–6th generations
  1. Etch enamel + dentin with 20–40% orthophosphoric acid for 20 s; wash off; careful drying (do not overdry!)
  2. Apply primer-bond mixture (or staged) for the recommended time; dry to remove solvent & distribute evenly — takes 30–90 s
  3. Light polymerisation 10–20 s (not needed if a chemical activator is present)
  4. Introduce freshly mixed amalgam and condense; remaining stages as in the classical method
  • 6th generation self-etching: no acid flushing step; light polymerisation mostly not done; curing under amalgam via chemical activator
  • Total-etch 4th/5th gen examples: Scotchbond MP · AllBond 2 · OptiBond FL · PQ Amalgam · Amalgam Bond (Plus) · One Step (Plus)
  • 6th gen multi-step self-etch examples: Clearfil Liner Bond 2V · FL-Bond · Nano-Bond
Variant 2 — modified Baldwin’s method
Orange — history & idea Baldwin (1897): kneaded amalgam is packed into a cavity whose bottom and walls are covered with a thin layer of freshly kneaded cement → better marginal adherence, adaptation, prevention of displacement. Originally zinc-phosphate cement dissolved in oral fluid → technique was not widely used. Interest returned with GICs, composite fixation cements and adhesive systems: they give chemical AND micromechanical adhesion (cement is incorporated into amalgam during condensation). Technique is poorly studied in vitro/in vivo but promising.
  • Any GIC can be used, but preference: hybrid (methacrylate-modified) dual-curing GICs — RelyX™ Luting Plus · FujiCEM™ · Protec Cem · PermaCem
  • Steps: ① isolation + matrix → ② thin insulating spacer up to enamel-dentin border, polymerise → ③ knead hybrid GIC + one-step amalgamation of capsule → ④ apply GIC thin layer on bottom & walls, condense amalgam → ⑤ remove excess cement; light-polymerise around the perimeter (dual-curing GIC) → ⑥ finishing
  • With dual-curing composite cements (Calibra · RelyX™ ARC · Variolink II · Twinlook · Nexus2 · Panavia F · BiFix DC): tooth tissue must be prepared — total etching + 4th/5th generation adhesive
  • Self-etching systems used less (low pH can impair cement polymerisation); exceptions — same-manufacturer pairs with patented catalysts: Xeno III + Calibra; OptiBond Solo Plus SE + Nexus2
  • Steps: ① isolation + matrix → ② dressing, priming, bonding per instructions, light-cure → ③ knead cement + amalgamate capsule one-step → ④ apply cement thin layer with applicator on all surfaces, place & condense amalgam → ⑤ remove excess; light-polymerise each wall around the perimeter for 40 s; carve → ⑥ finishing
⚠ The most important point In ANY variant — prevent contamination of the working field; contamination can negate all the advantages of the technique.

COPPER AMALGAM Cu 32–37% · pressed tiles

0/1 memorized ▼
Definition — grey thick-border box Copper amalgam is a solution of copper in metallic mercury, available as small pressed square tiles.
  • Composition: copper 32–37% · mercury 59–66% · zinc 2–4%
  • Zinc is added to reduce compressibility, improve mercury retention and colour stability

✓ “+”

  • Malleable
  • Little change in shape/volume after insertion
  • Filling does not move away from cavity edges
  • Considerable strength

✗ “−”

  • Does not stick to cavity walls
  • Stains tooth tissue darker
  • Good (i.e. bad) thermal conductivity
  • Slowly hardening
  • Causes corrosion of gold crowns (mercury released)
  • Less resistant to chemical attack in gingival cavities
Preparation rules (numbered process)
  1. Heat 2–3 plates or more in a special spoon over an alcohol/gas burner flame at 240–260 °C — mercury expands and loosens the amalgam
  2. After small mercury droplets appear on the surface (usually 5–10 s after heating), transfer to a glass mortar
  3. Rub thoroughly with force for 2 min with a pestle → homogeneous plastic mass (heat and rub for 2–4 min total, in the fume cupboard)
  4. Keep the mortar on the table (not in the air), placed in an enamelled tray — prevents mercury splashing
  5. Wash carefully with alkaline water to remove metal oxides: 5–6 drops of ammonia alcohol or 0.5 g baking soda per glass of water
  6. Store prepared amalgam in moist gauze in a jar with a lapped stopper
⚠ Overheated / underheated = ruined Overheated amalgam: significantly longer setting, easily disintegrates into small pieces. Underheated: stays hard, cannot be rubbed with a pestle. Either way it is not suitable for use.
Setting signs & application
  • Beginning of setting = shiny surface of the ball turns matt
  • Beginning of hardening (crystallisation) = amalgam lost plasticity, does not yield to rolling into a ball again
  • Application: filling carious cavities in molars and premolars; dental treatment in children
  • Nowadays almost all countries use silver amalgam with significant copper addition — high-copper amalgam

SILVER AMALGAM Ag 65–66% · filings + Hg

0/1 memorized ▼
Composition (filings)
  • Silver 65–66% · tin 29–32% · copper 2–6% · zinc up to 1%
MetalRole & imbalance effects
Silver↑ strength, ↓ fluidity, ↑ corrosion resistance. Excess → excessive expansion; lack → significant shrinkage
Tinaccelerates amalgamation. Excess → more shrinkage, less strength & hardness, longer hardening
Copper↑ strength, better adherence to cavity edges, ↓ fluidity. Too much → opposite effect
Zincprevents oxide formation, eliminates inclusions (binds oxygen & impurities); ↑ ductility, ↓ brittleness

✓ “+”

  • Higher hardness than copper amalgam
  • Good plasticity
  • Hardly stains tooth tissue (unlike copper amalgam)
  • Does not collapse in gingival-margin cavities

✗ “−”

  • No stickiness
  • High thermal conductivity
  • Can corrode gold crowns; galvanism
  • Greater shrinkage & volume change than copper amalgam
Preparation rules (manual technique)
  1. The coarser the filings, the slower they dissolve in mercury → grind/pulverise filings in a mortar for a few minutes BEFORE adding mercury
  2. Ratio: 3–4 parts filings : 1 part mercury (or as instructed)
  3. Grind filings + mercury in the mortar — friction of pestle on rough mortar walls generates heat that favours combining; if not rubbed thoroughly the mass stays brittle and porous, filling unstable
  4. Quality check: properly prepared amalgam should not crack when squeezed through a gauze cloth
  5. Before filling, squeeze out excess mercury until a crunch is felt — fingers protected with rubber gloves/gauze (don’t “salt” the amalgam, don’t drive mercury into skin pores); squeeze mercury into a jar with water (lapped stopper), jar standing in an enamelled tray with water
  6. Mercury waste → sealed container with potassium permanganate solution
⚠ Purple — hardening times Silver amalgam hardens for 1.5–2 h; the process is completed in 6–8 h. Finishing and polishing → at the second visit, after 24 h.
Application & representatives
  • Filling cavities of Black’s classes I, II, V with sufficiently strong walls; maxillary cavities of molars & premolars
  • Domestic: “Amadent” (Russia, disposable doses in capsules) · LZMP (50 g alloy + 50 g mercury P-1) · SSTA-01 (Ag 68%, Sn 28%, Zn 1%, Cu 3%)
  • Foreign: Amalcap Plus (Vivadent) · Vivacap n.g. (Vivadent) · Contour (Kerr) · Sybraloy 41% · Tytin (Kerr), etc.

GALLODENT-M — mercury-free alternative Ga instead of Hg

0/1 memorized ▼
Definition — grey thick-border box Gallodent-M is a gallium-based material for mercury-free metal fillings.
  • Powder: silver alloy mixture 94% + nickel 6%
  • Liquid: gallium-tin alloy
Work rules
  1. Take required powder & liquid with the special measuring cups → place in a polyethylene capsule
  2. Mix in an amalgam mixer for 20–30 s
  3. Properly prepared = consistency like a well-cooked squeezed amalgam
  4. Working time up to 3–4 min; end of curing at the 10th–15th min; processing only after 24 h
  • Filling technique similar to amalgam; pay special attention to pressure when placing
  • Gallodent-M fillings are not insulated
  • Application: paediatric practice — caries of varying activity, cavities of classes I, II, V of temporary and permanent teeth

Q1What is dental amalgam and what is its place in dentistry?▶
Amalgam is a self-hardening metal alloy obtained by mixing mercury and a powder containing different metals in certain proportions. It belongs to the class of materials for direct permanent filling. Amalgam is one of the oldest restorative materials in dentistry and serves as the benchmark for comparison with modern restorative materials. Its main purpose is to restore defects in the chewing group of teeth. Black, in his 1896 classification and proposed rules for the preparation of carious cavities, focused on amalgam as the main restorative material.
Q2Give the classification of amalgams.▶
Amalgams are classified in several ways. By powder composition: simple amalgams, which contain one metal, for example copper; complex amalgams, which contain two or more metals, for example silver, tin and copper; and noble amalgams, which contain noble metals such as palladium. By the base metal of the powder we distinguish silver amalgams, copper amalgams and Gallodent-M. Silver amalgams are divided generationally into traditional amalgam with the gamma-2 phase and modern amalgam without the gamma-2 phase; by particle type into needle or traditional, spherical, and mixed; by particle size into small, medium and large; by setting rate into quick-setting, medium and slow-setting; by the method of application into classical working technique and adhesive technique; and by form of release into encapsulated amalgam, powdered amalgam and pure mercury.
Q3What metals does amalgam contain and what does each of them do?▶
In addition to mercury, amalgam may include silver, tin, copper and zinc. Mercury is the actual primary agent causing the chemical reaction, but increasing its amount more than necessary reduces the strength and causes toxic and allergic reactions. Copper gives strength, is the basis of corrosion protection and gives antibacterial properties, but it increases volumetric expansion and accelerates curing. Silver gives strength and protects against corrosion, but causes volumetric expansion of the amalgam. Tin slows amalgam hardening and increases plasticity, but reduces strength. And zinc increases the mercury wettability of the components during alloy preparation. The composition ratio is approximately equal: 43 to 54 per cent mercury and 57 to 46 per cent powder, and most of the properties of amalgam are determined by the composition of the powder.
Q4Describe the properties of amalgam — advantages and disadvantages.▶
Amalgam has four groups of properties. Physical: high compressive and tensile strength, high radiopacity, good resistance to wear, no significant volumetric changes, and high thermal and electrical conductivity. Chemical: certain resistance to corrosion, stability of the internal structure — the amalgam phases — the presence of residual unreacted mercury, and reaction with oral fluid with possible staining of the filling and tooth tissue. Biological: tolerance to the oral mucosa and dental pulp. Operational: convenience and speed, sufficient working time, broad indications, and easy polishing with a long-lasting mirror effect. The advantages are durability — the average lifespan is about ten years — low cost, a simple and fast application technique, a wide range of clinical situations, the ability to repair large defects in a single visit, the self-sealing effect at the filling-tooth interface, and easy restoration when correcting old fillings. The disadvantages are the rigorous design of the carious cavity, which often requires removal of healthy tissue; the lack of adhesion to tooth tissue and the possibility of marginal adhesion failure; non-compliance with aesthetic requirements; low corrosion resistance of traditional amalgams; high thermal and electrical conductivity with potential galvanic effects; and the mercury content, which requires additional waste management measures.
Q5What are the indications and contraindications for amalgam?▶
Modern amalgams can be used for the treatment of medium to large carious defects of classes I, II and V in deciduous and permanent teeth, including areas subjected to high chewing loads; for Class V cavities in molars and premolars without high aesthetic requirements; for restoration of the tooth stump under artificial metal and metal-ceramic crowns; in patients with inadequate hygiene; in cases where it is difficult or impossible to control the dryness of the working field; and where price is the determining factor in choosing the filling material. The contraindications are allergy to mercury or any metal in the powder, and clinical cases in areas visible in the smile where high aesthetics is the main requirement — for example, treatment of anterior teeth, because of the risk of serious staining of the tooth tissue. For silver amalgam there are additional contraindications: gold structures in the oral cavity, teeth used for metal orthopaedic constructions, filling of the frontal group of teeth, radiation therapy in the maxillofacial region, cavities with thin walls, allergy to amalgam components, and diseases of the oral mucosa.
Q6Explain the phases of amalgam and its curing reaction.▶
An amalgam phase is a general term for the structure of an amalgam — the matrix formed by the interaction of the individual components, which are chemical compounds of metals. The gamma-phase is the main component of the powder before it is mixed with mercury: the intermetalloid of silver and tin, Ag3Sn. The gamma-1 phase is the compound of silver and mercury, Ag2Hg3, formed by amalgamation. The gamma-2 phase is the compound of tin and mercury, Sn8Hg, also formed by amalgamation. And the eta-phase is the result of the interaction between copper and tin in modern amalgams — the intermetallic alloy Cu6Sn5. In traditional amalgams based on the Ag3Sn alloy, silver is 65 to 75 per cent, tin 23 to 33 per cent, and copper usually 2 to 8 per cent, with traces of zinc and lead; up to 95 per cent of the powder is silver and tin, and after solidification the gamma-2 phase is present. In modern amalgams, the minimum copper content is 12 per cent and can reach 30, silver is 40 to 80 and tin 17 to 32 per cent; because of the higher copper, the gamma-2 phase is absent after solidification — these are also called copper-rich amalgams. The reaction proceeds initially in a uniform way: during amalgamation, crystallisation nuclei form a matrix of the interaction products of silver, tin and mercury. Not all Ag3Sn particles react with mercury — the unreacted particles are distributed in the amalgam and play the role of filler. In modern amalgams, copper does not interact with mercury but actively reacts with tin to form Cu6Sn5 crystals distributed in the material.
Q7What is amalgamation, and how are capsules used?▶
Amalgamation is the automatic process of mixing the basic components — mercury and powder — to form a plastic, self-hardening mass suitable for filling dental hard tissue defects. Manual mixing is not currently used due to the high risk of mercury intoxication. The capsule is a single-use release form of amalgam components that minimises the risk of contact with pure mercury; the powder-to-mercury ratio in capsules is approximately 1.0 to 0.85–0.96. Capsules are divided into active and passive. Active capsules do not require any additional actions before amalgamation: they have one section where the metal powder and mercury are located in a small thin polyethylene bag, and when the mixer is switched on, the bag is torn open by vibration and the components mix. Passive capsules have two sections containing the mercury ball and the powder, and they must be activated before mixing — the piston is pressed or the sections are rotated towards each other so that mercury contacts the powder. The amount of alloy is coded either by numbers from 1 to 5 or by the colour of the capsule lid, similar to endodontic instruments. For a small or medium Class I–II cavity in premolars one capsule No. 1 or No. 2 is usually sufficient; for molars, two or more No. 2 or No. 3 capsules are often needed; large capsules No. 5 are used rarely, mainly for MOD cavities.
Q8How should the cavity be prepared for an amalgam filling?▶
Because amalgam has no adhesion to tooth tissue, the cavity should be box-shaped with retention points and walls of sufficient thickness, with a flat bottom perpendicular to the direction of masticatory pressure and plumb walls. Sharp angles between the walls and bottom must be absent — they are rounded with pear-shaped burs to prevent fractures of the tooth walls and violation of marginal adhesion. If a low-silver Generation I amalgam is used, the angle between the bottom and the walls should be 90 degrees; with high-copper amalgams of the II and III generations without the gamma-2 phase, the walls are made slightly convergent and the angle is about 70 degrees to improve mechanical retention. The bottom should have no sharp corners, the height-to-width ratio should not be less than 2:3, additional retentions should hold the amalgam, enamel edges are bevelled at 45 degrees, carious tissue is removed extending the boundaries to immune zones, and enamel without dentin support must be removed. The cavity is formed within enamel and superficial dentin, at least 1 to 1.5 millimetres, and in areas of greater chewing load the filling should be at least 2 millimetres thick. Since amalgam is thermally and electrically conductive, the dentin over the whole cavity area is covered with an insulating pad 0.1 to 0.3 millimetres thick; glass ionomer cements are the best choice for this seal. For Class II cavities, isolation with a wedge-fixed matrix is necessary.
Q9Describe the technique and timing of filling with amalgam.▶
Before filling, we prepare the amalgam mixer and a minimal set of tools: a gun, one to three corkscrews of various sizes, and one or two carvers of different shapes. The mixer’s main characteristic is mixing speed, determined by oscillations per minute and amplitude — the amplitude of most machines is about 5 centimetres and the frequency varies from 2000 to 4000; below 2800 the amalgamation rate is low, from 2800 to 3500 medium, and over 3500 high. Mixing most often takes 8 to 12 seconds. Then several small portions of amalgam are introduced into the cavity with the gun and carefully condensed with a corkscrew so the material adheres tightly to the walls; subsequent portions may be larger. Condensation is usually done with medium force — more force is needed only with a significant proportion of needle-like particles, while spherical particles need low to medium force. Adequate condensation removes unreacted mercury and gives a monolithic, poreless filling. The material is applied with a small surplus. The standard operating time for filling and condensation is 3.5 to 5 minutes; the setting time of modern amalgam varies from 3.5 minutes for fast-setting to 6 minutes for slow-setting. The mixed amalgam is unsuitable if it is brittle and has lost its lustre. In Class II cavities the matrix is removed no sooner than 5 minutes, by gentle vestibular-oral movements, and large surpluses are removed before the matrix. Dryness of the field is desirable, but complete dryness is necessary only with zinc-containing amalgams, because zinc reacts with water, releasing hydrogen, which forms pores and expands the filling.
Q10How are carving and polishing of an amalgam filling done?▶
Carving is modelling the filling by removing excess amalgam with a carver after it has set, and we must always cut towards the hard tissue of the tooth. The average carving time is about 6 minutes. First, excess material is removed from the marginal ridge and the filling-tooth interface with sliding movements from the filling to the enamel to prevent chipping; movements should be precise and with little force, because in the first 10 minutes the amalgam is easy to work with but still quite brittle. Modelling follows four steps: trimming the edge of the filling with a Hollenbach instrument at 45 degrees, removing the matrix, removing excess amalgam from the interdental space, and modelling the masticatory surfaces based on the cusps with a Le Cron. Transitions and contact surfaces are modelled with sickle-shaped carvers, excess is removed with floss, and occlusion is checked with occlusal paper — first vertical, then lateral movements. The criteria that carving time has expired are a metallic creaking sound and the need for considerable force to cut. After 15 to 20 minutes from mixing, excess can usually only be removed with a polishing bur. Finishing aims at the smoothest, brightest surface resistant to plaque adhesion and corrosion, and there are two equally effective options. The classic option: on the first visit the surface is smoothed with a burnisher, and after 24 hours the filling is finished with silicone polishers and brushes with polishing paste — this gives better appearance and no mercury-rich surface layer. In the second option burnishing and polishing are done immediately, in one visit, which is recommended for amalgams with spherical particles. Amalgam polishers are silicone heads: brown first, as more abrasive, and green for the final polish; modern high-copper amalgams darken over time, and polishing with a brush and paste restores their lustre.
Q11What is the adhesive technique for working with amalgam?▶
The adhesive technique is the result of the search for a way to compensate for the disadvantages of amalgam. By using an intermediary — an adhesive system — problems such as lack of adhesion to tooth tissue and heat and electrical conductivity are eliminated, and the need for additional retention points is reduced, which preserves more healthy tooth tissue. Its disadvantages are higher cost and a complex, technique-demanding procedure. Preparation, condensation and processing are identical to the classical technique; the difference is in tooth preparation before amalgam application. Any enamel-dentin adhesive system gives amalgam chemical adhesion through the interaction of amalgam metals with carboxyl groups of the oxygen-inhibited layer formed after polymerisation of the adhesive. But this is only conditionally adhesive, because the bonding force often does not exceed 7 megapascals — clearly insufficient for amalgam’s clinical situations; the system mainly acts as an insulating spacer improving marginal adhesion. The first variant uses special adhesive systems of the 4th to 6th generations, total-etch and self-etching, which provide adhesion strength to dentin of more than 20 megapascals. The general scheme for 4th and 5th generation systems: etching of enamel and dentin with 20–40 per cent orthophosphoric acid for 20 seconds, washing off, careful drying without overdrying; application of the primer-bond mixture for the recommended time, typically 30 to 90 seconds; light polymerisation for 10 to 20 seconds, which is unnecessary if a chemical activator is present; then introduction and condensation of freshly mixed amalgam. With 6th generation self-etching systems there is no acid flushing step and light polymerisation is mostly not carried out. The second variant is a modification of Baldwin’s method, proposed in 1897: amalgam is packed into a cavity whose bottom and walls are covered with a thin layer of freshly kneaded cement. Zinc-phosphate cement dissolved in oral fluid, so the technique was not widely used, but interest returned with glass ionomer cements, composite cements and adhesive systems, which provide both chemical and micromechanical adhesion because the cement is incorporated into the amalgam during condensation. Preference is given to hybrid methacrylate-modified dual-curing glass ionomer cements such as RelyX Luting Plus, FujiCEM, Protec Cem and PermaCem; with dual-curing composite cements such as Calibra, RelyX ARC or Variolink II, the tooth is prepared with total etching and a 4th or 5th generation adhesive, and each wall around the perimeter is light-polymerised for 40 seconds. The most important point in any variant is to prevent contamination of the working field, which can negate all the advantages of the technique.
Q12What mistakes occur during treatment with amalgam?▶
Most errors in the use of amalgams are related to violation of technology or the wrong choice of material. First, the use of traditional amalgam is currently not the optimal solution to obtain a good clinical result. Second, incomplete preparation of the carious cavity creates conditions for the development of recurrent caries and endodontic pathology. Third, incorrect design of the prepared cavity often results in breakage of cusps and tooth walls, failure of the contact point, or loss of the filling. Fourth, insufficient or excessive amalgamation time reduces the strength of the amalgam and shortens the working time. And fifth, incorrect condensation technique leads to poor marginal fit, porous fillings and a high residual mercury content, and therefore a greater risk of corrosion and chipping of the filling.
Q13Tell me about copper amalgam.▶
Copper amalgam is a solution of copper in metallic mercury, available as small pressed square tiles. Its composition is copper 32 to 37 per cent, mercury 59 to 66 per cent, and zinc 2 to 4 per cent; zinc is added to reduce compressibility, improve mercury retention and colour stability. Its advantages are that it is malleable, changes little in shape and volume after insertion, does not move away from the cavity edges, and has considerable strength. Its disadvantages are that it does not stick to the cavity walls, stains the tooth tissue darker, has high thermal conductivity, hardens slowly, causes corrosion of gold crowns because mercury can be released from the filling, and is less resistant to chemical attack in gingival cavities. To prepare it, two or three plates are heated in a special spoon over an alcohol or gas burner flame at 240 to 260 degrees; mercury expands and loosens the amalgam. After small mercury droplets appear — usually 5 to 10 seconds after heating — the amalgam is transferred to a glass mortar and rubbed thoroughly with force for 2 minutes, with total heating and rubbing of 2 to 4 minutes, in a fume cupboard. Overheated amalgam disintegrates easily and sets much longer; underheated amalgam stays hard and cannot be rubbed — both are unsuitable. The mortar must stay on the table in an enamelled tray to prevent mercury splashing. Copper amalgam is washed particularly carefully with alkaline water to remove metal oxides — 5 to 6 drops of ammonia alcohol or 0.5 grams of baking soda per glass of water — and stored in moist gauze in a jar with a lapped stopper. The beginning of setting is when the shiny surface of the ball turns matt; the beginning of hardening is when the amalgam, having lost plasticity, no longer yields to being rolled into a ball. It is used for filling carious cavities in molars and premolars and for dental treatment in children. Currently, almost all countries use silver amalgam with a significant addition of copper — high-copper amalgam. Representatives are SR MOIT-58, STA-43, SMTA-56, tableted copper amalgam, and foreign ones: Vivacap, Amalgam GK alloy and Contour by Kerr.
Q14Describe silver amalgam — composition, preparation and representatives.▶
Silver amalgam contains metal filings and mercury. The filings consist of silver 65 to 66 per cent, tin 29 to 32 per cent, copper 2 to 6 per cent, and zinc up to 1 per cent. Silver increases strength, reduces fluidity and increases corrosion resistance — an excess leads to excessive expansion, and a lack to significant shrinkage. Tin accelerates amalgamation, but an excess increases shrinkage, reduces strength and hardness, and lengthens hardening. Copper increases strength and adherence to the cavity edges and reduces fluidity, but too much gives the opposite effect. Zinc prevents oxide formation and eliminates inclusions by combining with oxygen and impurities, increasing ductility and reducing brittleness. The advantages are higher hardness than copper amalgam, good plasticity, hardly any staining of tooth tissue, and no collapse in gingival-margin cavities. The disadvantages are no stickiness, high thermal conductivity, corrosion of gold crowns and galvanism, and greater shrinkage and volume change than copper amalgam. In preparation, the coarser the filings, the slower they dissolve in mercury, so they are pulverised in a mortar before adding mercury; the usual ratio is 3 to 4 parts filings to 1 part mercury. Grinding filings with mercury in the mortar is especially important: friction generates heat that favours combining, and if the mass is not thoroughly rubbed, the filling stays brittle, porous and unstable. Properly prepared amalgam should not crack when squeezed through gauze. Before filling, excess mercury is squeezed out until a crunch is felt, using gloves or gauze so as not to contaminate the amalgam or drive mercury into the skin; mercury is squeezed into a jar with water in an enamelled tray, and mercury waste goes into a sealed container with potassium permanganate solution. Silver amalgam hardens in 1.5 to 2 hours, the process completes in 6 to 8 hours, and finishing and polishing are done at the second visit after 24 hours. It is used for filling Class I, II and V cavities with sufficiently strong walls. Domestic representatives are Amadent, LZMP and SSTA-01; foreign ones are Amalcap Plus, Vivacap, Contour, Sybraloy and Tytin.
Q15What is Gallodent-M and how is it used?▶
Gallodent-M is a gallium-based material for mercury-free metal fillings. Its powder is a silver alloy mixture of 94 per cent with 6 per cent nickel, and the liquid is a gallium-tin alloy. The required quantities of powder and liquid, taken with the special measuring cups provided, are placed in a polyethylene capsule and mixed in an amalgam mixer for 20 to 30 seconds. The material is properly prepared when its consistency is similar to a well-cooked squeezed amalgam. Gallodent-M has good plasticity and hardens very quickly in the oral cavity: its working time is up to 3 to 4 minutes, curing ends at the 10th to 15th minute, but the material can only be processed after 24 hours. The filling technique is similar to that used for amalgam fillings, with particular attention to the pressure during placement, and Gallodent-M fillings are not insulated. In paediatric dental practice it is used in the treatment of caries with varying degrees of activity in cavities of classes I, II and V of temporary and permanent teeth.
